Bury banknotes in old bottles. Pay men to dig them up again. That was not a joke. It was a policy suggestion, from the most influential economist of the twentieth century. 
In 1936, in the depths of the Depression, John Maynard Keynes published The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money. Its promise was seductive: when the economy falters, government should spend, borrow and create demand. Recessions were no longer corrections to endure, but failures to manage from the center.
Friedrich Hayek argued that cheap credit does not create wealth. It falsifies interest rates and lures businesses into investments the real economy cannot sustain. The boom plants the bust. And in 1944 he warned that ever-wider economic planning leads step by step toward concentrated power. 
After the war, Keynes won. In 1965, Time put him on its cover under the line 'We are all Keynesians now.' In 1971, President Nixon told a reporter, 'I am now a Keynesian in economics.' That same year he closed the gold window. The last anchor on the dollar was gone.
The theory said inflation and unemployment trade off: The 1970s broke that promise. By 1980, consumer inflation hit 14.8 percent a year. Breaking it took interest rates near 20 percent and unemployment of 10.8 percent. Milton Friedman had predicted this breakdown in 1967. 
Keynes's most famous line comes from 1923: 'But this long run is a misleading guide to current affairs. In the long run we are all dead. Economists set themselves too easy, too useless a task if in tempestuous seasons they can only tell us that when the storm is long past the ocean is flat again.' 
Whatever he meant by it, 'the long run' became something politics could safely ignore. Nobel laureate James Buchanan showed how Keynesian ideas freed politicians from balancing budgets. Spending now wins votes. The bill arrives later, addressed to people too young to vote, or not yet born.
Washington has not run a surplus since 2001. Federal debt has passed 37 trillion dollars , more than the whole economy produces in a year. In 2024, interest on that debt cost more than national defense. And the dollar? Since the gold window closed, it has lost more than 85 percent of its purchasing power. That is a tax on savings no one ever voted for.
Hayek said the curious task of economics is to show men how little they really know about what they imagine they can design. Keynes promised to manage the storm. Instead, we inherited the debt. The long run is here. And we are the ones living in it.
